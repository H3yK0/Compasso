Many people notice a pattern: the slip comes after a bad day. An argument, pressure at work, hard news. It isn't a coincidence or a lack of character. Stress literally changes which parts of the brain are in charge.

## The part of the brain that brakes

The prefrontal cortex, the region just behind your forehead, is the most evolved part of the human brain. It's what holds back an impulse, remembers the plan, weighs now against later and chooses. It's your "smart brake".

In a review published in 2009, neuroscientist Amy Arnsten brought together the research on how stress affects this region. The central conclusion is striking: the prefrontal cortex is **the part of the brain most sensitive to stress**. Even **mild** acute stress, when it feels out of control, can cause a **rapid and dramatic** loss of prefrontal functions. With prolonged stress, there are even changes in the structure of the neurons in this region.

As the brake weakens, older, more automatic circuits linked to emotion and habit gain ground. In practice, the brain swaps "think first" mode for "react fast" mode. That's useful in the face of real danger; it gets in the way badly when the "react fast" you've learned is opening pornography.

## The detail about control

An important point in the research is **perceived control**. Stress that feels uncontrollable is what knocks the prefrontal cortex down the most. Feeling that you can do something, even something small, is protective.

> On a tense day, you didn't get weaker. Your brake got slower. Plan for that.

## What to do with this information

1. **Treat stress as a risk signal**, just like night and loneliness. A "heavy day" calls for a protection plan, not extra confidence.
2. **Decide beforehand, not during.** Decisions made calmly in the morning are worth more than ones made at 11 p.m. after a bad day. That's why Compasso's smart control exists: changes that loosen control only take effect 24 hours later.
3. **Give your body back a little control.** Slow breathing, with a long exhale, helps lower arousal (see the article on slow breathing). Two minutes in Breathe already count.
4. **Move.** A short walk or a few exercises discharge some of the tension and change the context.
5. **Find one small action on the problem.** Writing down what's weighing on you and a first concrete step reduces the sense of being out of control.
6. **Talk to someone.** Shared stress usually weighs less (see the article on connection).

## When stress is constant

If stress has become routine, with poor sleep, frequent irritability or a sense of exhaustion, it deserves care of its own. A health professional can help you deal with the cause, not just the effects. Taking care of stress is also taking care of your control.
