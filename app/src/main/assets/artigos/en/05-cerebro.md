Two exaggerations circulate about pornography: that it's harmless for everyone, and that it "destroys the brain". Research sits in the middle, with real findings and many questions still open.

## What the WHO recognizes

Since ICD-11, the World Health Organization's classification of diseases, there is **compulsive sexual behaviour disorder** (code 6C72). It isn't only about pornography, but it includes it. The core points:

- a **persistent** pattern of failing to control intense, repetitive sexual impulses;
- over a long period, **for example 6 months or more**;
- with **marked distress or impairment** in personal, family, social, educational or work life;
- repeated unsuccessful attempts to cut down, and carrying on despite the consequences.

An important detail: distress that comes **only** from a moral judgment about one's own desire isn't enough for the diagnosis. In other words, the yardstick is loss of control and harm, not guilt.

## What brain studies show

- A study of **64 men** found that those who watched more hours per week had a smaller volume in part of the reward system (the striatum) and a weaker connection between it and the prefrontal cortex. The authors themselves warn: **the direction can't be known**. It could be an effect of use, or an earlier trait that leads to more use.
- A study of men **in treatment** for problematic use showed that the reward system reacted more strongly to the **cues announcing** an erotic image, not to the images themselves. That's the pattern of cue-triggered "wanting", similar to what is seen in other compulsive behaviors, such as gambling.

## Feeling addicted also involves values

In a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults, about **11% of men and 3% of women** agreed, at least in part, with the statement "I am addicted to pornography". That feeling was linked to **how much the person uses**, but also to **religiousness** and to **moral conflict** about their own use.

This doesn't mean the problem is "all in your head". It means that **use and values weigh together**, and that too much shame tends to increase suffering, not reduce use.

## What isn't known yet

- Most studies capture **a single moment** and involve men: they show association, not cause.
- The effects seem to vary a lot from person to person.
- There is no test that "shows" an addicted brain.

> If your use is getting in the way of your life, wanting to change is legitimate, with or without a label.

The good side of all this: what the brain learns through repetition, it also relearns through repetition. Reducing cues, riding out urges and building other rewards is exactly that relearning.
