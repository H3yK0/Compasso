"I'll never make it." That sentence isn't just a sad thought: it changes what you try, how long you persist and how you react to the first stumble. Psychology has a name for its opposite.

## Self-efficacy

In 1977, psychologist Albert Bandura proposed the concept of **self-efficacy**: the belief that you **can** do what a situation demands. It isn't self-esteem (liking yourself) or general optimism. It's something more specific: "I'm able to do *this*".

Bandura argued that this belief influences which challenges a person takes on, how much effort they put in and how long they persist in the face of obstacles. And he described **four sources** it comes from:

1. **Mastery experiences:** having succeeded before. This is the strongest source.
2. **Vicarious experiences:** seeing someone similar to you succeed.
3. **Verbal persuasion:** hearing from someone credible that you're capable.
4. **Body states:** how you interpret a racing heart, tension, tiredness.

## Why starting small works

If the experience of succeeding is the strongest source of confidence, the strategy becomes clear: **set up situations where you can win**. Giant goals ("never again") fail easily, and every failure teaches "I can't". Goals of the right size teach the opposite.

> Confidence isn't what you feel before succeeding. It's what's left after succeeding many times.

## How to apply it every day

- **Pick the right level.** On Lodestar, start with Easy. Finishing a whole session, using "I'm close" when you need to, is a mastery experience. Move up a level when your last sessions feel solid; the app suggests when.
- **Count small wins as wins.** An urge you rode out, a night with the phone out of the bedroom, an honest check-in.
- **Use examples of people who made it.** Support groups also work because you see people like you moving forward.
- **Choose the voices around you carefully.** Someone who believes in you, without pressure, helps more than a thousand motivational videos.
- **Reinterpret your body.** A racing heart during training isn't a sign of failure; it's exactly the moment to use the pause and breathe.

Each small win is concrete proof, stored in memory, that you're capable. They add up.
