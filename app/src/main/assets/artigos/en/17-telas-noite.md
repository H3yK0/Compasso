For many people, the riskiest moment is the same every day: lying down, lights off, phone in hand. There are two problems there at once. One is the obvious trigger, the screen one tap away. The other is less visible: the light from the screen itself messes with the body clock.

## The experiment

Researchers at Harvard compared, in a controlled setting, two ways of reading before bed: on an **e-reader with a lit screen** and in a **printed book**. The same people went through both conditions, on consecutive nights, reading in the hours before going to bed.

With the lit screen, participants:

- **took longer to fall asleep**;
- felt **less sleepy in the evening**;
- produced **less melatonin**, the hormone that signals night to the body;
- had their **body clock delayed**;
- and woke up **less alert the next morning**.

The authors point out that screen light is rich in short wavelengths (the well-known bluish light), which the body reads as "it's still daytime".

## The cycle this creates

Put the pieces together: the screen delays sleep; you sleep less; the next day you're more tired; a tired brain makes worse choices (see the article on sleep and movement); at night, your guard is even lower. And the phone is still there, in bed.

> Many relapses don't start with a decision. They start with "I'll just take a quick look before sleeping".

## A plan for the night

1. **Charge your phone outside the bedroom.** It's the simplest change and one of the most powerful. A cheap alarm clock takes care of waking up.
2. **One screen-free hour before bed**, or as much as you can. Start with 20 minutes and build up.
3. **Swap the ritual:** a paper book, a warm shower, stretching, calm music, conversation.
4. **If you use a screen at night, lower the brightness and use night mode.** It helps a little, but it doesn't replace distance.
5. **Read on Compasso earlier**, not in bed. Even the readings here are a screen.
6. **Regular times to sleep and wake up**, weekends included, help the body clock settle.
