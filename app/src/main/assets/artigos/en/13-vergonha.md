After a slip, it's common to feel a weight that's hard to name. Sometimes it's guilt; sometimes it's shame. They seem alike, but psychology treats them as different emotions, with very different effects on what you do next.

## The difference

Psychologist June Tangney and her colleagues sum it up like this:

- **Guilt** focuses on the **behavior**: "I did a bad thing". The discomfort points to something that can be repaired.
- **Shame** focuses on the **self**: "I am bad". The discomfort points to who you are, and that feels impossible to fix.

In their review of moral emotions, the authors bring together decades of studies showing the upsides and downsides of each. Guilt, when it isn't mixed with shame, tends to lead to **reparative actions**: apologizing, owning up, changing something. Shame tends to lead to **hiding, denying or escaping**, and sometimes to anger and defensiveness.

## Why this matters here

With pornography, shame often closes a loop:

1. You slip.
2. Shame arrives: "I'm weak, dirty, hopeless".
3. To avoid feeling that, you isolate and hide.
4. Alone and feeling bad, the search for quick relief grows.
5. You slip again, and the shame grows.

The problem isn't feeling something after a slip. Feeling that you didn't act in line with what you value is useful; it shows that it matters to you. The problem is when the feeling becomes a verdict on your worth as a person.

> "I did something I didn't want to" opens the door to change. "I'm garbage" shuts the door and turns off the light.

## How to turn shame into something useful

- **Change the sentence.** Swap "I am" for "I did". "I'm an addict and I'm weak" becomes "last night I was alone with my phone at two in the morning and I slipped".
- **Look for the point of repair.** What can you do now? Adjust the plan, move the phone somewhere else, schedule time with someone.
- **Break the secret, carefully.** Telling a trusted person, a group or a professional usually lightens the weight of shame a lot. It grows in the dark.
- **Watch out for borrowed shame.** Many people carry old messages that sexual desire is dirty. Wanting to change a habit doesn't require hating yourself for having desire.
- **Use kindness as a tool** (see the article on self-compassion). It doesn't erase responsibility; it puts you in a position to take it on.

## A sign to seek help

If the shame is constant, or comes with thoughts that you're worthless or that there's no way out, talk to a mental health professional. That isn't overreacting: it's caring for the part of you that most needs care right now (see the article on getting help).
