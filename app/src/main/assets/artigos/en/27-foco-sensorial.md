People who have spent a lot of time with pornography sometimes find that real sex has become strange: too fast, or hard to feel, or full of pressure. **Sensate focus** is one of the oldest techniques in sex therapy, designed precisely for this: relearning how to feel.

## Where it comes from

The technique was developed by researchers William Masters and Virginia Johnson, and it has been part of sex therapy for more than four decades. The central idea is simple and, at the same time, counterintuitive: **take the goal out of touch**. With no goals of erection, penetration or orgasm, attention stops going to performance and returns to sensation.

## How it's usually done

In general, the practice is proposed in stages, agreed on in advance by the couple:

1. **Touch without the genital areas.** One person touches, the other receives, and then they switch. The instruction is to pay attention to your own experience of touching or being touched (temperature, texture, pressure), not to pleasing or arousing.
2. **Touch including the genital areas, still with no goal.** If arousal comes, fine; if it doesn't, that's fine too. Nothing needs to "happen".
3. **Gradually, more freedom**, always with the agreement that either person can pause and go back to an earlier stage.

The point isn't to follow a perfect script, but to switch off the inner judge (see the article on performance anxiety). When the pressure disappears, the body usually responds more naturally.

## What research says

A 2016 critical review points out that sensate focus is widely used, but that **research on its effectiveness is still limited**, especially outside the group Masters and Johnson studied (heterosexual couples, without disabilities and highly educated). The authors also note that much of the practical knowledge about the technique circulated only in professional workshops, which led to different interpretations. In other words: it's a well-established clinical tool, not a sure-fire solution, and it works best as part of professional care.

> Feeling isn't a test you pass. It's something you practice.

## If you don't have a partner

The same logic applies to solo practice: touch with attention on sensations, **without pornography**, without hurry and without the goal of reaching the end. That's the spirit of Compasso's training: perceive your body, notice arousal rising, pause and breathe.

## When to seek guidance

A sex therapist or a psychologist trained in sexuality can adapt the stages to your situation and help with whatever comes up along the way, such as anxiety, conflicts in the couple or pain. If there's pain or physical difficulty, see a doctor too.
