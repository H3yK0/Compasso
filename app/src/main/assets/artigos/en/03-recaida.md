Almost everyone who changes a habit slips at some point. The problem is rarely the slip itself. The problem is what comes right after: *"that's it, I ruined everything, so who cares"*.

## A slip is not a relapse

Psychologist Alan Marlatt, one of the creators of relapse prevention, separated two things:

- **Slip (lapse):** a one-off stumble, once.
- **Relapse:** going back to the old pattern, or giving up on the goal.

A slip raises the risk of relapse, but **doesn't lead to it automatically**. Between one and the other there is a choice.

## The "since I already blew it…" effect

Marlatt described the *abstinence violation effect*: after the slip come guilt and shame, and the person explains what happened as a fixed flaw of their own ("I'm weak", "I'm hopeless"). People who think this way are more likely to drop the goal, even just to escape their own guilt.

Those who see the slip as **a specific situation they didn't yet know how to handle** ("I was exhausted, alone, with my phone in bed") are more likely to learn from it and keep going.

> The useful question isn't "why am I like this?". It's "what happened before, and what will I do differently next time?".

## What to do in the next 24 hours

1. **Stop attacking yourself.** Too much guilt doesn't protect you; it pushes you toward the next slip.
2. **Investigate like a detective:** time, place, emotion, what came before.
3. **Change one thing in your plan.** Just one, and concrete (see the article on triggers).
4. **Get back to your routine today:** check in tomorrow, breathe, play, read. Don't wait for "Monday".
5. **If slips are frequent**, that's information, not a verdict: it may be time to ask for help (see the article on professional help).
