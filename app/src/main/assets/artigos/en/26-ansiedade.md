"What if I can't?" "What if it's over too fast?" "What if my partner notices?" These thoughts, in the middle of sex, have a name: **sexual performance anxiety**. And they tend to bring about exactly what they fear.

## The size of the problem

In a 2020 review on the subject, researcher R. E. Pyke gathered representative population surveys and estimated that sexual performance anxiety affects **9% to 25% of men** and **6% to 16% of women**.

In men, it **contributes to premature ejaculation** and to **psychogenic erectile dysfunction**. In women, it can **strongly inhibit desire**. According to the author, performance anxiety causes or maintains most of the common sexual complaints, and yet there is no formal diagnosis for it, which has left research on treatment scarce.

## Why anxiety gets in the way

Sexual arousal and the threat alarm pull the body in opposite directions. Arousal needs a relatively relaxed body and attention on sensations. Anxiety switches on alert mode and pulls attention outward: toward evaluation, judgment, "how am I doing".

Therapists William Masters and Virginia Johnson called this **spectatoring**: instead of feeling, the person starts watching themselves, like a judge in the audience. And a judge on the lookout for failures usually finds them.

> The harder you try to "make it work", the less room there is for your body to work on its own.

## What helps

- **Drop the performance goal.** Sex isn't a test. Moments of intimacy with no goal of penetration or orgasm lower the pressure (see the article on sensate focus).
- **Bring your attention to sensations.** Temperature, texture, breathing. When your mind drifts to judging, come back gently, as in mindfulness.
- **Breathe slowly** before and during. A long exhale helps turn the alarm down.
- **Talk.** Telling your partner about the fear usually lightens it a lot (see the article on talking about sex).
- **Train calmly, on your own.** Compasso's stop-start training helps you learn your own rhythm with no audience and no pornography.

The review notes that **cognitive behavioral therapy** and **mindfulness training** work well for performance anxiety in general and are recommended for the sexual kind, although specific controlled studies are lacking.

## When to see a professional

If the difficulty is frequent, started suddenly or comes along with erection problems, it's worth talking to a urologist or family doctor, to rule out physical causes, and to a psychologist or sex therapist. Treatments exist, and it's the professional who recommends and follows them.
