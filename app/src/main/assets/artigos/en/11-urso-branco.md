Try it now: for one minute, **don't think of a white bear**. For almost everyone, the bear shows up within seconds, and comes back, and comes back. That little game is the basis of one of the best-known experiments in psychology, and it has everything to do with urges.

## The experiment

In 1987, psychologist Daniel Wegner asked students to say out loud everything that went through their minds for five minutes while trying **not to think of a white bear**. If the bear appeared, they rang a bell. It appeared anyway, many times: no one managed to suppress the thought as asked.

Then came the most interesting part. Afterwards, the same students were allowed to think about the bear. And they thought about it **more** than another group that had been invited to think about the bear from the start. Trying to push the thought out seemed to make it more present later, an effect that became known as **rebound**.

In a second experiment, people who were given **a specific thought to use as a distraction** during suppression had less rebound afterwards. "Don't think of X" wasn't enough. What helped was having **a concrete Y** to take the attention to.

## Why this happens

To manage "not thinking" about something, the mind has to keep checking whether it has shown up. That vigilance keeps the target itself lit. It's like trying not to look at a stain on the wall: you know exactly where it is.

Something similar happens with sexual urges. Phrases like "I can't think about it, I can't, I can't" turn the urge into the center of attention. And an urge at the center of attention is exactly the kind that grows (see the article on waves).

> Fighting the thought hands it the microphone. Giving your attention another task takes the microphone away, calmly.

## What to do instead

1. **Don't argue with the thought.** Name it and let go: "this is an urge, it comes and goes". No arguing, no repeating "no".
2. **Have a "Y" ready before you need it.** Something concrete that occupies your mind: a song to sing all the way through, a puzzle, a game in the Refuge, counting backwards by sevens.
3. **Move your body somewhere else.** Getting up, turning on the light and drinking water changes the context and gives your attention new things to process.
4. **Return to the Y as many times as needed.** The thought will come back; that's normal. Each calm return to the Y is a training rep, not a defeat.
5. **Once it passes, write down the trigger.** The goal isn't to never have the thought again, but to learn what calls it.

## An honest caveat

These experiments used neutral thoughts, in a lab, and the rebound effect doesn't always show up with the same strength in every study. But the practical lesson held: forbidding a thought tends to work worse than **redirecting your attention** to something specific. That's what Lodestar's Refuge tries to offer: a place to take your mind when it insists.
