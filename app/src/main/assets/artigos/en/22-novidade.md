Why is it so hard to stop scrolling, even when nothing there is actually good? One of neuroscience's most important discoveries helps explain it, and shows why endless, unpredictable content is so gripping.

## The prediction error

In the 1990s, neuroscientist Wolfram Schultz recorded the activity of dopamine neurons in monkeys during tasks with rewards, such as a little juice. In 1997, with Peter Dayan and Read Montague, he published a synthesis that changed the way dopamine is understood:

- When the reward comes **without warning**, the neurons fire.
- Once the animal learns that a signal (a light, a sound) announces the reward, the neurons start firing **at the signal**, no longer at the reward.
- If the signal appears and the expected reward **doesn't come**, activity **drops** below normal.

In other words, these neurons don't simply signal "pleasure". They signal **prediction errors**: the difference between what you expected and what happened. It's a learning system, built to pay attention to what's better than expected.

## Why novelty hooks you

Combine this with the article on dopamine (wanting isn't the same as liking) and the picture becomes clear. An environment where the next thing **might** be better than the last, and you never know when, keeps the prediction system on alert. Every scroll is a small bet.

Infinite feeds, short videos and pornography sites work exactly like that: endless, unpredictable novelty. This is an interpretation based on basic neuroscience, not an experiment on pornography itself, but it helps explain why "just one more" turns into an hour.

> What hooks you isn't what you find. It's the chance of finding something better in the next one.

## How to take unpredictability out of the way

1. **Decide before opening.** "I'll check the weather forecast and close it" is an intention with an end. "I'll take a look" has no end.
2. **Cut infinite feeds** at risky times: apps off the home screen, notifications off at night.
3. **Let boredom be boredom** for a few minutes, without chasing the next novelty (see the article on boredom).
4. **Prefer predictable, good rewards:** a book, a conversation, a walk. They don't set off the same alarm, so they don't pull the same way.
5. **Wait.** Lodestar's smart control spaces out releases so that hurry stops deciding for you.
