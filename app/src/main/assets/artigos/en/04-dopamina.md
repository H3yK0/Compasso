Online, dopamine has become the villain: "the pleasure molecule", "dopamine detox", "videos fry your dopamine". Science tells a more interesting story, and a more useful one.

## Wanting is not the same as liking

Researchers Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson spent decades separating two things that seem the same:

- **Liking**: the pleasure you feel in the moment.
- **Wanting**: the force that pulls you toward something.

Dopamine is much more tied to **wanting** than to liking. In animal studies, removing dopamine ended the motivation to go after food, but the pleasure reactions to tasting something sweet stayed normal. And raising dopamine made "wanting" grow without increasing "liking". In people, lowering dopamine reduced the desire to use a drug, but not how pleasurable it was.

## Why this matters to you

With repetition, the **cues** tied to a habit (the time of day, the dark bedroom, the phone in your hand, certain images) gain the power to grab your attention and trigger desire. It's *wanting* pulled by the cue, even when the pleasure itself is no longer much.

That's why so many people describe the feeling of **wanting more and more while liking it less and less**. It isn't a moral weakness: it's the motivation system responding to the cues it has learned.

> You don't need to "reset" your dopamine. You need to change the cues and what comes after them.

## What to do in practice

- **Cut the cues:** phone outside the bedroom, notifications off at night, old shortcuts deleted.
- **Add friction to the old path** and keep the new one within reach (Compasso on your home screen, for example).
- **Teach yourself to wait:** smart control spaces out releases precisely so that "wanting" stops running the clock.
- **Look for other rewards:** movement, people, music, learning something, finishing a task. The motivation system still needs a target; give it good targets.
- **A literal "dopamine detox" doesn't exist:** what works is reducing the cues and repeating the new path until it becomes the default.
