Below the bladder and around the base of the penis there's a group of muscles almost nobody knows by name: the **pelvic floor**. It supports the pelvic organs, helps hold in urine and takes part in erection and ejaculation. Like any muscle, it can be trained. That's what's called a **Kegel** exercise, after gynecologist Arnold Kegel, who popularized it in the late 1940s.

## What studies show

- **Erection:** in a clinical trial in the United Kingdom, **55 men** with erectile dysfunction were randomly assigned to pelvic floor exercises with professional guidance and biofeedback (plus lifestyle advice) or to lifestyle advice alone. At 3 months, the exercise group improved much more on the erectile function score. After the other group also did the training, **40%** of participants reached normal function and **34.5%** improved.
- **Premature ejaculation:** in an Italian study of **40 men** with lifelong premature ejaculation, 12 weeks of pelvic floor rehabilitation led **33** of them to gain control, with average time reaching about **two and a half minutes**. There was no comparison group.
- **The bigger picture:** a systematic review of **10 studies** concluded that pelvic floor training seems effective for erectile dysfunction and premature ejaculation. But the studies are of low to moderate quality, and **there is still no optimal protocol**.

In other words: it's promising, cheap and safe training for most people, but not a guarantee. It works best as part of broader care.

## Why it helps with control

When arousal rises, the pelvic floor takes part in the ejaculation reflex. A stronger and, above all, **better-known** muscle helps you notice that point earlier. It's the same skill Compasso's stop-start training tries to build: notice early, pause, breathe.

> Strength helps. But what matters most is knowing where the muscle is and being able to let it go.

## How to train the right way

1. **Find the muscle** by imagining you're holding in pee or gas. Use that only to find it: don't train by stopping your pee midstream.
2. **Squeeze by pulling in and up**, without tightening your belly, buttocks or thighs.
3. **Breathe normally** while you hold.
4. **Let go completely** between one squeeze and the next. Relaxing is half the training.
5. **Start light:** short squeezes, lying down. Increase the time little by little, as Compasso's Kegel does with its hold levels.
6. **Be consistent:** a little every day is worth more than a lot once in a while.

## When not to do it on your own

If it hurts, stop. Pelvic pain, pain when peeing, pain during sex or trouble relaxing the area may point to a muscle that's **too tense**, and then squeezing more doesn't help. In those cases, see a doctor or a **pelvic floor physiotherapist**.
