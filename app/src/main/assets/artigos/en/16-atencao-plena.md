"Mindfulness" has become a buzzword, and that sometimes hides the fact that there's serious research behind it. One of the most cited studies in the addiction field tested whether training attention helps prevent relapse after treatment.

## The study

Researchers at the University of Washington followed **286 people** who had just completed initial treatment for alcohol or other drug use. They were randomly assigned to three kinds of aftercare, with 8 weekly group sessions:

- **Cognitive-behavioral relapse prevention** (RP): identifying risky situations and practicing responses.
- **Mindfulness-based relapse prevention** (MBRP): the same ideas, plus practices of observing sensations, thoughts and urges without reacting on autopilot.
- **Treatment as usual**: the follow-up the clinic already offered.

Everyone was followed for a year, with self-reports and tests.

## What they found

- At **6 months**, both relapse prevention groups (RP and MBRP) had a **lower risk of relapse** than treatment as usual. The cognitive-behavioral approach had the edge in delaying the first drug use.
- At **12 months**, the mindfulness group reported **fewer days of use** and **fewer heavy drinking episodes** than the other two.

One possible reading: planning techniques help a lot at the start, and the skill of observing an urge without obeying it seems to last longer.

## Why it may help with pornography

The study is about substances, not sexual behavior, and that needs to be clear. But the mechanism being trained is the same one you need when an urge hits: **noticing the impulse early, without fighting it and without obeying it**. Research with smokers showed something similar when teaching people to "surf the urge" (see the article on waves).

> Mindfulness isn't emptying your mind. It's seeing a thought as a thought, not as an order.

## A short practice for everyday life

In mindfulness groups for relapse, a pause of a few steps is used in the middle of real life. A simple version:

1. **Stop.** Interrupt whatever you're doing for a few seconds.
2. **Observe.** What's happening in your body? Heat, tightness, restlessness? What thoughts are passing through?
3. **Breathe.** A few slow breaths, with a longer exhale.
4. **Widen.** Look around: where are you, what time is it, what matters to you today?
5. **Respond by choice.** Now decide the next step, ideally one from your plan.

Practicing when everything is fine too, five minutes a day, keeps the skill ready for when the urge hits. Compasso's Breathe is a good place for that.
